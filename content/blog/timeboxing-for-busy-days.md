You open your inbox to answer a message. There's another request beneath it, an attachment to check, and a conversation you need to find. Half an hour later, the task you sat down to do is still waiting.

Timeboxing means giving an activity a fixed time budget and deciding what happens when that budget ends. The key is to choose the limit before the task expands. “I'll spend twenty minutes answering the messages needed for tomorrow” gives you a boundary that “I'll catch up on email” doesn't.

You can use a [timeboxing timer](/timeboxing-timer) for the countdown. First, give the countdown a useful job.

## Write the stopping rule before pressing Start

A timebox needs three things: an activity, a duration, and a result you can review. Without the third, it's easy to restart the timer indefinitely and call that a plan.

Consider a small purchasing decision. You need to compare three printing suppliers for an event. Your stopping rule could be: record the total price, delivery date, and one unresolved question for each supplier. Allow 30 minutes to gather that information.

If all three options meet the requirement after twenty minutes, you can stop and decide. If a supplier hasn't published a delivery date by minute thirty, mark it as missing and decide whether to send a question. You don't need another ten open tabs to explain what remains unknown.

The budget limits the attempt. It doesn't guarantee that the task will be complete or make a rushed answer acceptable.

## Timeboxing, time blocking, and Pomodoro have different jobs

Time blocking reserves a place in the calendar: “09:30–10:00, supplier comparison.” Timeboxing defines the amount of time you're willing to spend and the review at the end. You can use both on the same task.

Pomodoro organizes repeated focus periods and breaks. It can provide the rhythm inside a timebox, but a timebox doesn't have to last 25 minutes or repeat. A ten-minute inbox check and a 40-minute research pass are both valid budgets if they fit your situation.

If you're mostly choosing a work-and-break rhythm, the [25/5 versus 50/10 comparison](/blog/pomodoro-25-5-vs-50-10) covers that decision. If you need to stop a task from using the whole afternoon, begin with its scope.

## A worked example: thirty minutes of inbox triage

Suppose you have 30 minutes before a meeting. You need the inbox to reveal what matters, not reach zero.

1. **First five minutes:** scan for messages that affect today's commitments. Flag those that need action. Leave newsletters and routine updates for another time.
2. **Next twenty minutes:** answer the bounded questions you can resolve with the information available. Turn larger requests into named tasks instead of starting them inside the inbox.
3. **Final five minutes:** record the remaining actions, send any necessary acknowledgment, and prepare for the meeting.

For a complex message, “Check the revised delivery figures before replying to Sam” is a better task than “Email Sam.” It names the work that prevents a quick answer.

This example assumes you can defer nonurgent messages. If your role requires live responses, keep the required channel available and choose a timebox that allows interruptions.

## Give uncertain work a narrower question

Research and writing often resist a simple finish line. A useful timebox doesn't need to promise the finished article or the final technical answer.

For research, ask one question and define what you'll collect: three possible causes of an error, each with a way to check it. For writing, draft one section without editing the rest of the document. For planning, list the decisions that must be made before estimating the project.

If you reach the end with a clearer question, record it. That is a result you can use to choose the next attempt. “Still researching” gives the next block much less direction.

## Decide what to do when the time is up

At the end, save your work and choose among three actions:

- **Stop:** the result is sufficient for its purpose.
- **Schedule another attempt:** there is a specific missing piece worth more time.
- **Change the approach:** the task is blocked, poorly scoped, or needs someone else's input.

An extension should have a new reason and a new boundary. “Ten more minutes to verify the second quote” is different from restarting because stopping feels uncomfortable. If every attempt runs over, check the scope and estimates during a [weekly project review](/blog/weekly-project-review).

## Use a short template for the next task

Copy this into your notes and fill it in before starting:

> Task: Compare the three printing quotes.  
> Time budget: 30 minutes.  
> Result to inspect: Price, delivery date, and open question for each.  
> Outside this attempt: Redesigning the event materials.  
> At the end: Choose a supplier or send the missing question.

Open the [30-minute timeboxing timer](/timeboxing-timer), or change the duration to suit your task. Check automatic-start settings if you want to review before another interval begins. Leave space for a break afterward; the work budget and the whole appointment are different amounts of time.

For your first attempt, choose a task that tends to spread. Write the stopping rule in one sentence. When the countdown ends, use that sentence to decide what deserves your next block.
