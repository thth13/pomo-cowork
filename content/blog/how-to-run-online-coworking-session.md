You and a friend have both said you need to get some work done. You arrange a call, catch up for twenty minutes, talk through your plans, and leave with the same unfinished tasks. It was a good conversation. It just wasn't the work session you meant to have.

An online coworking session needs a small amount of structure: an agreed start, a task each person can do independently, a quiet work period, and a clear finish. You can work on completely different projects. The shared commitment is to show up and spend that time working.

Here's a practical way to run the first one without turning it into another meeting.

## Agree on the kind of company you want

Before inviting anyone, decide whether you're arranging quiet company or collaboration. Those appointments need different plans.

If you need a colleague to explain a spreadsheet before you can use it, schedule that conversation first. If you already understand the work and would like someone else around while you do it, a coworking block fits. One person can draft an application while another sorts receipts. Neither has to supervise the other.

Also decide where you'll speak or write. A brief message at the beginning and end may be enough. Video is optional; silence doesn't need a camera pointed at it.

## Use a one-hour agenda that leaves room to arrive

For a first session, try this example:

1. **Minutes 0–5: arrive and name the task.** Each person gives a short answer: “I'm checking the figures on the first two slides.” Open the materials you need.
2. **Minutes 5–50: work quietly.** Start a 45-minute countdown. Keep questions that can wait in a note beside your work.
3. **Minutes 50–55: leave the desk.** Get water or take a short walk around the room.
4. **Minutes 55–60: check in and finish.** Say what changed, what remains, and whether another appointment would be useful.

This is a suggested agenda, not a special timer mode. The [timer for working together](/timer-working-together) starts with a 45-minute focus block. Its ten-minute break can cover the final two parts of this example. You still manage the arrival and check-in yourselves.

If you prefer two shorter work rounds, use two 25-minute blocks with a five-minute break between them. That leaves only five minutes for both setup and the final update. Don't borrow that time twice.

## Send an invitation people can act on

A useful invitation answers when, where, how long, and what to bring. For example:

> Want to work alongside each other on Thursday, 14:00–15:00 Europe/London? Bring one task you can do on your own. We'll use the first five minutes to settle in, work quietly for 45 minutes, then take a break and send a short update. Let's confirm we can both access the room beforehand.

Use a calendar invitation that resolves the time zone for each person. Copy the room destination from the room you've agreed to use, and check access before the appointment. A link doesn't override membership requirements.

The [virtual coworking room guide](/virtual-coworking-room) explains how room access and recurring sessions fit together. Pomo Cowork doesn't book the appointment or send calendar invitations for you.

## Keep the opening goal small enough to start

“Work on my website” leaves several decisions unresolved. “Write the opening paragraph for the pricing page” gives you a place to put the cursor.

Choose a result you can attempt without waiting for another participant. If a task depends on a reply, name a fallback before the quiet period begins. You might prepare the comparison questions while waiting for a missing quote.

Avoid promising a finished result just to make the opening sound confident. “Try the first approach and write down where it fails” can be a useful goal for uncertain work.

## Decide what happens when somebody gets interrupted

Each person controls their own Pomo Cowork timer. Starting, pausing, or joining a room does not synchronize everyone else's countdown.

For a short interruption, pause your own session and return when ready. For something that takes you away, send a brief message and keep the group's agreed finish time. A late arrival can use a shorter interval or join the next round.

If you're working with a team, agree on an urgent-contact channel. Quiet time should never depend on guessing whether a genuinely important question is allowed.

## End with a useful update

Keep the closing answer close to the work: “The figures match now. I still need a source for the last slide.” That's enough information to make the next step clear.

Nobody needs to explain every minute or compare how much they produced. Different tasks have different amounts of uncertainty. For a recurring project, carry that next step into a [short weekly review](/blog/weekly-project-review).

Before arranging a longer session, ask whether the first one gave everyone enough quiet time. If it did, repeat the same simple format. If conversation kept taking over, give it its own slot before or after the work. You can [work together online](/work-together-online) without making the whole afternoon a call.
