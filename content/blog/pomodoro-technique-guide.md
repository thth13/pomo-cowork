The Pomodoro technique isn't complicated: work in focused chunks, break in between. The classic version is 25 minutes on, 5 minutes off, with a longer break after every fourth round. What actually makes it useful isn't the exact numbers — it's the boundary. You decide what you're working on *before* you start, and then that's the only thing you're allowed to think about until the timer goes off.

You don't need to plan this out perfectly before trying it. Pick something, open the [Pomo Cowork timer](/), and use your first round just to see how much actually fits into 25 minutes. You'll probably be surprised either way.

## Pick a task that has an actual end point

"Work on the project" is a bad starting task — not because it's wrong, but because it doesn't tell you what to do first. Swap it for something with a visible finish line:

- Write the opening paragraph of that proposal
- Read one section and jot down three takeaways
- Reproduce the bug and write down the exact steps
- Get through today's must-reply messages

It doesn't need to take exactly 25 minutes — some tasks run short, some spill into a second round. What matters is that you know the next physical action to take. If the task feels too big to even look at, don't try to estimate the whole thing. Just grab the first useful piece of it.

## Setting up your first round

Before you hit start: close the tabs you don't need open, and mute whatever notifications can wait 25 minutes. Keep a scrap of paper or a notes app nearby — not to write essays, just to park stray thoughts.

1. Write down the one thing you're about to do.
2. Open the [online Pomodoro timer](/) and start a focus session.
3. Work on that thing. Only that thing.
4. If something else pops into your head, jot a few words and go back to the task.
5. When it ends, write a quick note on what changed, then take your break.

Think of the timer as protecting your attention, not as a promise that you'll finish everything by the end of it. Even a session where you realize your task was badly scoped counts as useful — now you know to break it down differently next time.

## When you actually get interrupted

Some things can genuinely wait. A message, a new idea, a task that isn't on fire — those go in your notes and get dealt with at the break. You're not ignoring them, just delaying by twenty minutes.

Other things can't wait, and pretending otherwise doesn't help anyone. If something urgent comes up, pause the session, deal with it, then decide whether to pick the timer back up or start fresh. Running a timer while you're mentally somewhere else isn't really a Pomodoro session — it's just a clock.

If the same interruption keeps showing up session after session, that's a signal to change something about the setup — phone in another room, a heads-up to a colleague, a shorter interval that fits between your meetings better.

## What a real break looks like

Get up. Get water. Look at something that isn't a screen for a few minutes. Pick something with a clear stopping point, so coming back to work doesn't require its own decision.

One habit worth building: before you step away, write a single sentence about where to pick up. "Continue from the second example" saves you a good two or three minutes of re-reading everything you just wrote.

Every few rounds, take a longer break — however long actually fits your day. Just don't let the break turn into its own to-do list. Its whole job is to be a pause, not a second work session in disguise.

## The interval length isn't fixed

25 minutes is a reasonable place to start, not a rule. If a task feels hard to even begin, a shorter interval — 15, sometimes 10 — can lower the barrier enough to get moving. If the work needs more runway before it's useful to stop, a longer one might suit it better.

Give one setting a fair few sessions before changing it again. Pay attention to whether you can actually start, stay on the task, and come back after the break — that tells you more than counting how many sessions you racked up compared to someone else.

And some work just doesn't fit this shape — meetings, live support, anything that needs continuous attention. Save timed blocks for the parts of your day where you get to choose one thing and protect it.

## Trying it with other people

If working solo makes it hard to start, an [online coworking room](/rooms) can help more than people expect. Everyone picks their own task, everyone works during the same block. Agree up front whether you'll check in afterward or just work quietly in parallel — either works.

Keep any check-in short: what you meant to do, what actually got done, what's next. The point is a clean start and a clean stop — not turning the conversation itself into the interruption.

## Look at the work, not just the clock

At the end of a block, ask yourself what actually moved. A paragraph written, a problem you understand better now, a next step you can name — that's the win, not the fact that a timer rang.

Consistently finishing way less than planned? Make the next task smaller. Finishing early with time to spare? Use it to review what you just did, or pick a closely related next step rather than jumping somewhere unrelated.

Your first session doesn't need a plan beyond one clear task and a place to stop. [Open the timer](/), pick that one thing, and give it the next 25 minutes.
